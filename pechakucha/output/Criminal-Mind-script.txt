Forensic Psychology: How a Mind of a Criminal Works
PechaKucha, 10 slides, 20 seconds each

1. Why?   [0:00]
A man walks into a shop and robs it. The police ask how. Which door? What weapon? What time? A forensic psychologist asks something else. Why him? And why that day? For the next three minutes, I want to ask that question with you.

2. The Brake   [0:20]
First, the brain. In 1848 an iron bar shot through Phineas Gage's head. He lived, but friends said he was not Gage any more. He turned rude and impulsive. The front part is our brake. In one study, murderers' scans showed a weaker brake.

3. No Fear   [0:40]
Deeper inside is the amygdala, our alarm. It reacts when we see a frightened face. In people with psychopathic traits it tends to stay quiet. They see your fear but don't feel it. It is information, like a weather forecast. And punishment doesn't scare them much.

4. The Mask   [1:00]
A psychopath wears a mask. Charming outside, no guilt inside. The diagnosis is dissocial personality disorder, and the psychopath is its coldest type. About one in a hundred, but up to one in five in some prisons. Most never kill anyone. Some do well in business.

5. Born? Made?   [1:20]
So, born or made? Both. A New Zealand study followed children from birth. Boys with a risky gene and an abusive childhood got into the most trouble. The same gene in a safe home did almost nothing. Genes load the gun. Childhood pulls the trigger.

6. Signature   [1:40]
Back to our shop. A profiler tries to read the scene like handwriting. Gloves and a broken window are the method. He needed them. Anything he didn't need is his signature. A profile gives no name. At best, it makes the list shorter.

7. False Memory   [2:00]
Then the witnesses. But memory is not a camera. Loftus and Palmer showed people a car crash on film. When the question said smashed, people remembered more speed. Some remembered broken glass that wasn't there. And with a gun, people watch the gun, not the face.

8. Confession   [2:20]
Next, the interview room. The classic American method starts from guilt and pushes until the suspect confesses. It works too well. Innocent people confess too, especially teenagers. Police in England now ask open questions and listen. Then they check the story against the evidence.

9. In Court   [2:40]
Finally, the court. The psychologist helps with two questions. Did he understand what he was doing? Is his illness real, or is he acting? Later, in prison, a third. Is it safe to let him out? It is only an estimate, and somebody must sign it.

10. Earlier   [3:00]
So, why him? A brain with weak brakes. Little fear. A childhood that taught him violence. Why that day? One bad day. None of this excuses the crime. But much of it can be noticed early. We study it to get there before the police.
